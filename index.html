Enrichment isn't the reward for the work. It's part of it.

There's a child in every school who's never been out of the county, walked in a city or felt sand between their toes on a beach.

Not neglected, not in crisis. Just quietly growing up inside a small map of what's possible for someone like them. The trip that takes that child over the county line isn't a day out. It's the afternoon their sense of what they might do with their life gets bigger.

And it goes deeper than the trip. The child who does something hard on a Saturday and comes back changed by it carries that into Monday's lessons. They pick the harder question. They have another go when it doesn't work. Enrichment doesn't sit beside the academic work. It's often what unlocks it.

Here's the part that should bother us. When enrichment gets treated as a nice-to-have, it's the most disadvantaged children who lose it first, because for them the school's offer was the only offer going. Manage it as an add-on and it never gets planned properly and you never notice who's quietly missing out.

Enrichment isn't the reward for the real work. It's part of the real work.

So, honestly: which child in your school is the one your enrichment offer is quietly leaving out?

Five areas. Most schools are strong in two.

Most people hear "enrichment" and picture the chess club and the ski trip. Fair enough. But the DfE framework breaks it into five areas, and once you see them, you can't unsee the gaps.

Arts and culture. Sport and physical activity. Nature, the outdoors and adventure. Civic engagement. Wider life and future skills.

Try it as an exercise, honestly, on a Sunday night with a cup of tea. Most schools I bet would come out strong on two, decent on one, and thinner than they'd like on the other two — while cheerfully describing themselves as "good at enrichment". Finding the evidence to support this judgement is much harder. I include myself in that. The framework has a way of stopping you flattering yourself and genuinely identify your provision gaps — forcing you to dig deeper.

Because here's who the gaps belong to. Every school has a child. You can describe them: quiet, watchful, not sporty, not arty, on the periphery — the kind who without care slips through a timetable without ever being pushed. Put that child on a hillside with a map and a job to do and they might come alive. But if "nature and outdoors" is one Duke of Edinburgh cohort and not much else, that child doesn't get a thin version of it. They get nothing.

That's what the DfE framework does. Not grade the school; if utilised correctly it helps you find the child. It's easy to run a packed programme in your strong area, feel busy all year, and never notice the whole area where a particular kid would finally shine, sitting quietly empty.

Most of us could name our strong area in a heartbeat. It's the other question that's harder, and it's the one that matters and it has made me reflect: if you split your offer across the five DfE categories, which one would you least want that quiet child or a parent to challenge you on?

The word we hide behind

"We put it on. They didn't take it up."

I've said a version of that sentence. Most heads have. It sounds like fairness. It's usually a place to hide.

Earlier this week I wrote about the gap between two children three miles apart, and dozens of you replied. One comment has stayed with me all week, so here's the second post it pushed me to write. The moment we call enrichment an "offer," we've quietly given ourselves permission for some children not to have it. An offer, you can opt out of. An entitlement, you can't.

And this has almost nothing to do with money.

Give me an extra £50k tomorrow and I still couldn't turn an offer into an entitlement by Friday.

Money helps. I won't pretend otherwise. But I've led well-funded schools with a thin offer, and skint ones with an offer that genuinely changed lives. The difference was never the budget. It was whether the quiet child who'd never put herself forward got encouraged to participate anyway, or got left in the column marked "we did put it on."

Cash buys the minibus. It doesn't buy the culture that fills it at 5pm on a wet Thursday evening, and it doesn't buy the intention to go and find the child who'd never have chosen it for herself. That's a leadership job, and a slow one.

So a question, because your replies this week taught me more than my post did. Not about budgets. About honesty: what's the sentence your school hides behind, the one that sounds like fairness but quietly lets a child off the list?

The quiet part, out loud

As the year closes, and before the public story of my school pivots to results - I'm spending my time evaluating the year and planning for the next. Reflecting. Analysing and contemplating areas for improvement.

Dylan Wiliam says every teacher needs to improve, not because they're not good enough, but because they can be even better.

What strikes me this July is how many quietly did exactly that - no announcement, no public recognition - just better than they were in September.

I'm so incredibly grateful to my team for that effort. Without doubt it will be reflected in results. But the work that silently underpinned it all happened in a thousand unglamorous moments nobody measured.

The quiet word in a corridor that stopped a bad week becoming a bad term. The child who walked in this morning only because someone made them feel worth walking in for. And being that emotionally available adult who took the time to allow the disclosure to happen that will change the course of a child's life forever.

None of it shows up in the data.

All of it is the reason the data moves.

None of my incredible team did it to be seen. There was never going to be a moment of public acknowledgement for it. They did it because a child in front of them needed it, and that was reason enough.

That's the part of this job the measures will never reach.

So before the results land and the story of my school becomes a number again, I want to say the quiet part out loud.

The grades will come, and I'll be proud of them. But they are only ever the visible edge of something far larger - a year of people choosing to be better when no one was counting, for children who will never know the difference someone made.

That choice isn't in any framework. You can't mandate it, and you can't measure it. It comes from something the job can't issue and a scorecard can't capture: people who decided the child in front of them was worth more than the recognition they'd never receive.

That's the work. That's the year. That's the part I wanted to say out loud, before results day makes the story about something else entirely.

A retention crisis in disguise

In 2024, 15 teachers left our school. This September, for the first time, we have no teaching vacancies at all.

Secondary teacher job adverts are down 32% in a year - the lowest in a decade. And only 61% of teachers now expect to still be in the classroom in three years. Before the pandemic, it was 75%.

The whole national conversation is about drawing people into the profession. Almost none of it is about why good people walk out. Yes, it's pay. Yes, it's workload. But there's a cause we badly under-rate: teachers leave buildings where teaching has quietly become a war of attrition.

In 2024 we lost the retention battle on scale. Let me add some context: we were two years into transforming the school. I was asking my team to buy in. To hang on. To give me time, and trust that the behaviour would improve and the grind would ease. That the change would arrive.

Four years on, it has. Our latest staff survey shows most intend to stay at least another year. We have stability. The difference is what we now talk about. We talk about learning. They've stopped bracing themselves for period 5.

My summary of retention is simple: you cannot keep teachers in a school where teaching is a fight they have to win alone. You must cultivate a behaviour culture grounded in clear routines with leaders who hold the line so staff don't have to. The least glamorous, most important work we do.

If you lead a school: what's the one thing that made a teacher stay this year?

Behaviour policy vs belonging

Lately, I've been reflecting on behaviour in my school and where we go next. Over the past three years, we've built systems. Sanctions. Scripts. Rewards. We've worked hard to embed consistency, and rightly so. These things matter. But I've come to believe that something matters even more: belonging.

As my good friend Victor Allen says, "Behaviour is not something to be managed, but something to be understood." That line has stayed with me. Because when we take time to understand, we begin to connect, and from connection, trust grows.

So how do we take this forward? Belonging isn't something you capture in a poster or a policy document. It's a feeling. The kind a child carries in their stomach as they walk through the gates. The kind that says: you are safe here. You are known here. You matter here.

You can have the most comprehensive behaviour policy in the world and still have students who feel school isn't for them. Structure without connection. Compliance without commitment. Silence without safety. And in the process, we risk losing the very students who need us most.

This coming year, we're intentionally cultivating a culture of belonging. Not through slogans or one-off projects, but through the everyday rhythms of school life. It will live in how people are greeted, how voices are heard, and how differences are recognised and respected. It will be seen in the consistent, quiet actions: staff who notice, students who support each other, leaders who hear what is unsaid.

That's the shift we're making. Not from behaviour to belonging, but from managing to understanding, from reacting to relating. It will be slower. Harder. But it's the work the next stage of our journey will be built on. Because when students feel they belong, they don't just behave better. They believe more deeply in themselves, in others, and in what's possible.

That sense of belonging can't stop at the school gates. It has to extend to our staff, our parents, and our wider community too. And that's the next frontier.

Enrichment is school or nothing

For disadvantaged children, enrichment is school or nothing.

Wealthier families buy it anyway. Clubs, instruments, coaching, holidays. For everyone else, if the school doesn't lay it on, it doesn't happen.

And yet "extra-curricular" is the first thing to go. Call something "extra" and you've already half-decided it's expendable. First out of the budget, first off the timetable, first to disappear from a child's week the moment staffing gets tight.

From September, Ofsted starts looking at enrichment as part of personal development, and it becomes visible to parents on school profiles. The new £132.5m Every Child Can programme puts money behind it too. Both are welcome. Neither will be enough on its own.

What actually decides it is culture. I've watched children work out who they are on a pitch, in a rehearsal, on a chessboard, backstage. Rarely at a desk. And when a child finds the thing they're good at, the attendance and the behaviour and the sense of belonging tend to follow it in.

So enrichment can't be the bit you get to if there's energy left on a Friday. It has to be part of what the school is actually for. The rehearsal after school. The minibus at 8am on a Saturday.

Here's my question for other leaders, with budgets as tight as they are:

What's the one piece of enrichment you'd protect before anything else, and why that one?

Rigour vs relentlessness

In school leadership, it's easy to confuse rigour with relentlessness.

We assume being rigorous means increasing intensity, driving harder, pushing faster, and adding more layers to what we already do. Yes this can, under some circumstances drive rapid improvement but in my experience, intensity burns people out. Consistency builds them up.

True rigour looks different. It isn't about doing more, it's about doing what matters consistently well and repeating.

Throughout my leadership journey and particularly as a Principal leading schools through challenging circumstances, I've underpinned leadership behaviours with a shared language and a replicable, steady, sustainable rhythm of daily activity. This approach generates the rigour but embraces sustainability — leaving intensity for the moments when it is really needed.

At Cliff Park, we talk a lot about Everyday Excellence. Excellence is achieved through consistency and that repetition gives people confidence.

As that level of consistency flows through every layer of leadership, we have been able to harness a slow yet relentless momentum for school improvement.

That's really the heart of leadership rigour — repeated consistency in every interaction and intensity in bursts when required.
