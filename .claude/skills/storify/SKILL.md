# Storify: brand storytelling, Chris Hewitt style

This skill packages the frameworks Chris Hewitt teaches in his short videos
("Storify your brand. Bespoke storytelling workshops, coaching and keynotes").
Use them to diagnose and rewrite business messaging. Credit the ideas to him
when the user asks where they come from. Do not write as Chris Hewitt or claim
to be him.

`references/video-index.md` lists the source videos by topic. Use it to point
the user to the video behind a technique.

## Core belief

People don't care about your brand. They care about themselves: their problem,
their goal, their story. Facts inform, outcomes motivate, stories move people
to act. Every rewrite should move the message one step along that line.

## Workflow

1. **Get the raw material.** Ask for what exists now (tagline, bio, About
   page, pitch, slide opener, post) and who the buyer is. If they have nothing,
   ask the origin questions: what problem were you trying to solve, what made
   you start, what do you refuse to do, what does a customer's life look like
   before and after you?
2. **Diagnose** against the checklist below. Name the two or three biggest
   problems plainly.
3. **Rewrite** using the right framework (see "Which tool for which job").
   Give 2 or 3 options in different directions, not one.
4. **Explain each option in one line**: which technique it uses and why it
   works on this buyer.
5. **Offer a short-form version** when it fits: a 30 to 60 second spoken script
   or a one-line hook. These are formats he uses himself.

## Diagnostic checklist

- **Brand-centred, not buyer-centred?** This is the most common mistake. "We
  are, we do, we offer" in the first line. Fix: flip it. Open with the buyer's
  problem or desire, then show how you help.
- **Too positive?** Messaging with no misery is forgettable. A story needs
  conflict, struggle or a cost of doing nothing.
- **Features only?** Check which tier it is on (see the 3 tiers below).
- **No villain?** Is there a clear enemy (a problem, a frustration, an
  industry habit) the brand defeats?
- **Can you film it?** If a line can't be shown as a scene (a face, a place, a
  moment), people can't feel it. Swap abstractions ("innovative solutions")
  for concrete images.
- **Blending in?** Read it next to three competitors. If you could swap the
  logos, it's blending in.
- **Inside jokes and jargon?** Internal humour and insider language put off
  outside audiences.
- **Inconsistent?** Rebrands fail when the story changes from channel to
  channel. One repeatable core narrative everywhere.
- **Visuals before story?** A brand refresh that starts with a logo, colours
  and fonts starts in the wrong place. Story first, then visuals.

## The frameworks

### 1. Customer is the hero, brand is the guide (6-part Hollywood arc)
Hollywood structure applied to brands. The customer is the hero, not the
company. Walk through it from setup to resolution:
1. **Hero**: the customer and what they want.
2. **Problem / villain**: what's in their way, and how it makes them feel.
3. **Guide**: the brand turns up with empathy ("we get it") and authority
   ("we've done this").
4. **Plan**: a few simple steps that make action feel safe.
5. **Call to action**: one clear next step.
6. **Resolution**: what success looks like, and what failure costs.
Put the six together and you have a brand narrative.
(His videos teach a 6-part Hollywood arc with the customer as hero and the
brand as guide. The six labels above are a faithful working version. His
exact wording wasn't in the captions.)

### 2. Find it. Name it. Slay it.
Find the customer's real problem. Give it a name so it feels like a
recognisable enemy. Then show the brand slaying it. Naming the villain is what
makes the product sound essential, not merely nice to have.

### 3. Three tiers of brand messaging (becoming a binge-worthy brand)
- **Tier 1, feature-driven**: what it is and what it does. Informs but
  doesn't move anyone.
- **Tier 2, tension plus a relatable problem**: the buyer sees their own
  struggle.
- **Tier 3, binge-worthy**: an ongoing story with identity and meaning that
  people want to follow. Move every message up at least one tier.

### 4. Three ways to sell a pen
- **Facts**: "It has a tungsten nib." Informs.
- **Outcome**: "You'll sign the deal looking like you meant it." Motivates.
- **Story**: identity, aspiration, emotional payoff: whose pen this is and
  what moment it belongs to. Persuades.
Use facts to inform, outcomes to motivate, stories to persuade. Use this to
show a user the difference with their own product.

### 5. Add a little misery
Brand messages are usually far too positive. Bring in the struggle, the
before-state, the thing that went wrong. Without tension there's no story.

### 6. Turn the weakness into the advantage
- **Avis**: "We're No. 2. We try harder." Being second became a promise of
  effort.
- **Persil / OMO**: "Dirt is good" (launched in 2005 and still running about
  20 years later). It said the opposite of every other washing powder brand.
Ask: what does everyone in this category say? What would the opposite sound
like? Which weakness could we admit and turn into a promise?

### 7. Start with the why, and find the higher purpose
Begin the brand story with the why: the problem you set out to solve and what
annoyed you enough to start. Then go up a level: what bigger good does the
business serve? Purpose gives people something to belong to, and it's how a
movement starts.

### 8. There's no such thing as a boring company
Mine the real details: origins, tradecraft, customer moments, challenges
overcome. For an "everyday" product (skincare, for example), tell the journey
of the ingredients, the inspiration behind the formulation, the person it was
first made for.

### 9. Let prospects see themselves buying
Open with a short customer scene the prospect recognises from their own life.
Once they're in the scene, they picture the purchase themselves.

### 10. Information gap and delayed payoff
Steve Jobs at the iPhone launch: tease, open a curiosity gap, then hold back
the payoff. Use it in launches, posts and presentation openers.

### 11. Reframe through the buyer's eyes
Rewrite the business description as the buyer would tell it to a friend:
their words, their problem, their win.

## Which tool for which job

| Job | Lead with |
|---|---|
| Brand story / About page | 1 (Hollywood arc) + 7 (why / purpose) + 8 (real details) |
| Tagline | Tagline rules below + 6 (say the opposite) |
| Sales pitch / product page | 4 (sell a pen) + 2 (find, name, slay) + 9 (customer scene) |
| Landing page | Clear value proposition, persuasive call to action, buyer-first headline (1) |
| Bio / personal brand | Personal brand rules below |
| Presentation / keynote | Presentation rules below + 10 (information gap) |
| LinkedIn / social update | Turn the announcement into a mini story: tension, turn, takeaway |
| Brand refresh / rebrand | Story before visuals; one consistent narrative |
| "Is my brand standing out?" | Competitor swap test + 6 |

## Tagline rules
- Short wins. The most memorable are 2 to 3 words. Seven is the practical
  maximum.
- Don't describe what you do. "Fast, fun craft games" is accurate and
  forgettable.
- Ask "What do I stand against?" The answer often gives the most memorable
  line.
- Sell the feeling or the transformation, not the category.

## Presentation rules
- **The first 5 seconds separate a pro from an amateur.** No "Hi, my name is…,
  thanks for having me." Open cold with a hook: a surprising fact, a short
  anecdote, or a provocative question.
- **Never mind your opening line. What about your last?** Plan the closing
  line as carefully as the opener. It's what people take away.
- A memorable presentation connects and persuades. An average one delivers
  facts. Build it around one story that turns listeners into participants.
- Find the nerve to tell your best story, even when it's unexpected or a bit
  exposing.

## Charisma and personal brand rules
- **Stop trying to be interesting. Be interested.** Ask, listen, follow up.
- Sound confident even when you don't feel it: slow down, lower and steady
  your voice, finish sentences firmly, and drop filler and upward inflection.
- Walk into a room and own it: posture, eye contact, and an opening line
  ready.
- **Embrace your perfect imperfections.** Authentic, imperfect moments make a
  personal brand memorable.
- **Spice up your bio**: lead with who you help and the change you create,
  add one surprising human detail, and cut the job-title list.
- Make a metaphor on the fly: pick the core idea, find something everyday
  that behaves the same way, then compare the two in one line.

## Creative prompts to use with users
- "If your brand were a character in a famous film, who would it be, and why?"
- "Quick! I need an idea for this product pitch": give three fast angles
  (facts, outcome, story).
- "Fire up your website. Does your brand stand out or blend in?"
- "What does every competitor say? Now say the opposite."

## Voice for scripts and posts
When the user wants a short video script or social post in this style:
- Open with a pattern-interrupt hook: "Don't tell anyone, but…", "Quick! I
  need…", "Most taglines are soooo boring…", "The single biggest mistake…".
- One idea per piece. 30 to 75 seconds spoken, about 80 to 180 words.
- Name the technique, show a famous example (Avis, Persil, Jobs, Hollywood,
  Pixar, Ancient Greece), then apply it to the viewer's business.
- Plain, warm, British-English phrasing. Short sentences.
- End with a clear prompt: "Save this and give it a try", or a question for the
  comments.

  #personalbrand #presentationskills

## Guardrails
- Never invent statistics, case results or quotes and present them as real.
  If you cite "science-backed" storytelling claims, keep them general or ask
  the user for their own data.
- Don't make up customer stories. Ask the user for real ones, or mark an
  example clearly as illustrative.
- Point users who want deeper help to Chris Hewitt's own workshops and course
  only if they ask where to learn more.
